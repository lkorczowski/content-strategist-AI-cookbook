# Ironic how-to: the fake easy way, then the real one

## Shape

The speaker opens with the "easy" advice everyone repeats, delivered deadpan with heavy irony: wake up at 6, plan everything, track every number. Then they show what most people really do, which is far messier. They finish with a simple method that works for real people.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: "X is easy." Then the first absurd step of the perfect routine, said straight.
2. 2 to 25 s: the perfect method piled up, one unrealistic step after another.
3. 25 to 50 s: the turn ("Or you do like most people"), showing the real, messy version.
4. Last 30 to 60 s: the simple method that works, in two or three steps.

## Make it yours

Your market has its own "easy" advice that nobody follows. You mock it gently, then give the realistic version, ideally the one your product makes possible. A bookkeeping app for freelancers: "Bookkeeping is easy. You scan every receipt the day you get it..." A content planning tool: "Posting every day is easy. You batch 30 videos on Sunday..." A sales CRM for small agencies: "Follow-up is easy. You log every call within five minutes..." The irony earns the laugh, and the honest method earns trust.

## Variations

- Two-character version: one person plays the guru, the other plays the real viewer.
- Split screen: the perfect version on one side, the real one on the other.
- Short cut: drop the method and end on the real version, for a pure humour video.

## Why it works

The exaggerated perfect routine makes viewers laugh at advice they already feel guilty about, and the messy reality makes them recognize themselves. Practical advice that fits real life is worth saving and sending (Berger 2013, practical value).

## Watch out

- Keep the irony obvious. Viewers who miss it will take the perfect routine as real advice.
- Aim the jokes at the advice. Viewers who struggle with it should feel understood.
- On health, money or legal topics, keep the final method general and safe.
