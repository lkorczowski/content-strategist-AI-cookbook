# Dream client turned nightmare

## Shape

The founder tells, to camera, how a dream client arrived, why it felt like a big break, what went wrong step by step, and what they learned. The client is never named or identifiable. It is filmed where the work happens, with a few cutaways to the work itself.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the contrast in one sentence ("when [a big name type] contacted us, it became my biggest disappointment").
2. 2 to 25 s: the dream. Who reached out (described by type only), the excitement, what was at stake.
3. 25 to 70 s: the slide. The first warning sign, then what went wrong, in order.
4. Last 15 s: the lesson and what you do differently now.

## Make it yours

You pick a real story from your company where a prestigious deal turned bad, and you tell it with the lesson your buyers can use. The lesson is the point: it shows how you work and what you protect. Examples: an AI agency founder on the enterprise pilot that changed scope every week, a SaaS founder on the large customer who asked for a custom version and then left, a dev tools founder on the influencer partnership that went wrong.

## Variations

- Lesson first: open on the rule you now follow, then tell the story that taught it.
- Two parts: the dream in part one, the nightmare in part two, posted a day apart (Zeigarnik 1927).
- Text version: the story told as a photo carousel.

## Why it works

The opening contrast promises a fall, and viewers stay to learn how it happened (Loewenstein 1994). Other founders recognize their own bad client and share it, which carries the video to your peers.

## Watch out

- Never identify the client: no name, no logo, no detail that lets someone guess.
- Check your contracts for confidentiality clauses before telling the story.
- Take your share of the blame. A story where only the client is wrong sounds bitter and scares future clients.
