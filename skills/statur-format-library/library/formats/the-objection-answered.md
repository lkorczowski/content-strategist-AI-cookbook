# The objection, answered

## Shape

The founder says out loud the objection buyers raise most, or the doubt people have about the business itself. Then they answer it with one fact and one example. The video ends on what the viewer should take from the answer.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the objection, in the buyer's own words, as a question ("Isn't that a terrible business idea?").
2. 2 to 20 s: the first answer. One fact that reframes the objection.
3. 20 to 70 s: one example that proves the fact: a customer case, a test, a number from your data.
4. Last seconds: the takeaway in one sentence, and an invitation to post the next objection in the comments.

## Make it yours

Your sales call notes are the source: list the objections you hear every week and make one video per objection. An AI meeting assistant answers "Is my data used to train your model?" with its data policy and one example. A no-code app builder answers "Won't I outgrow it?" with a customer who scaled on it. A cybersecurity tool for small teams answers "We're too small to be a target" with one public incident at a company of that size. Each video becomes a link your sales team can send.

## Variations

- Doubt about the business: the founder answers why the company makes sense at all (see the example).
- Comment version: the objection shown as a real comment on screen.
- Customer answer: a customer answers the objection in their own words, with permission.

## Why it works

Hearing your own doubt said aloud makes you stay to hear the answer, which turns a reason to leave into a reason to watch. An objection answered in public also helps the people who never ask it on a call.

## Watch out

- Answer the real objection, even when it is uncomfortable. A dodge loses the buyer.
- Every fact and example must be true and checkable.
- Customer examples need the customer's permission.
