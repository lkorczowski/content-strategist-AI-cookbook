# Too good to be true? Expert product test

## Shape

An expert opens with the product's claim that sounds too good to believe, for example a price far below the only similar device. They test it on real use, in their own setting. They give a verdict that names what holds up and where the limits are.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the claim, read out loud or on screen, with a skeptical face.
2. 2 to 50 s: the test. The expert uses the product the way their audience would, and shows each check on camera.
3. 50 to 120 s: the surprise. What actually works, then the limits, with a concrete example of each.
4. Last 10 s: the verdict in one sentence and who the product is for.

## Make it yours

Your product has a claim people doubt. Hand it to an expert in your buyers' field and let them test it in public. You can also play the expert yourself if you are known in the field, as long as you test the claim honestly and keep the limits in. Examples: an AI transcription tool tested by a court reporter on a noisy recording, an AI bookkeeping tool for freelancers tested by an accountant on a messy month, a code review assistant tested by a senior engineer on a real pull request.

## Variations

- Head to head: the cheap product against the expensive reference.
- Short review: one claim, one test, one verdict in 30 seconds (see the YouTube example).
- Customer as expert: a power user runs the test.

## Why it works

The claim opens an information gap ("is this real?") that only the verdict closes (Loewenstein 1994). The limits make the verdict credible, so the positive part lands as practical value.

## Watch out

- Disclose any commercial collaboration with the platform's label.
- Keep the limits in the edit. A test with no downside reads as an ad and loses the expert's credibility.
- Only quote claims your product actually makes, word for word.
