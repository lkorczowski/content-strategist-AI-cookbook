# Breaking down my own mistake

## Shape

The founder admits a public mistake in the first line ("Yes, that's me who..."). They replay it on screen, then break it down step by step: the context, the decision, and what they would do now. The post-mortem is published openly.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the admission, with the mistake already on screen.
2. 2 to 30 s: the replay. The moment it went wrong, shown as it happened.
3. 30 to 120 s: the breakdown. Context, the decision you made and why, what it cost.
4. Last 15 s: what you would do now, and the one lesson for the viewer.

## Make it yours

Pick a mistake people already know about, or one you can show with screenshots. A founder of an AI writing tool breaks down the launch that got no signups, with the landing page replayed. A founder of a B2B payments startup breaks down the deal they lost, with the anonymized email thread. A founder of a mobile app breaks down the bad hire, with the job post and the decisions around it. Showing how you think when things go wrong builds more trust than a success story.

## Variations

- Screen replay: the mistake shown as a screen recording with your voice over it.
- Greenscreen: screenshots of the mistake behind you, pointed at one by one.
- Customer mistake: a mistake you made with a customer, told with their permission and their view.

## Why it works

An admission in the first line surprises viewers who expect founders to show only wins, and the replay makes them want to see what went wrong. The breakdown gives them a lesson they can use, which turns a confession into practical value (Berger 2013).

## Watch out

- Own the mistake fully. Blaming others in the breakdown kills the format.
- Anonymize customers, candidates and partners unless they agree to appear.
- Check legal exposure before discussing a dispute that is still open.
