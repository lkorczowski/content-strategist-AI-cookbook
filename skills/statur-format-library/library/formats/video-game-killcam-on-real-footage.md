# Video game killcam on real footage

## Shape

First-person footage of a real activity (a race, a sales day, a sprint at work) is edited like a video game. Every small win triggers a game announcer sound and adds one to a kill counter on screen. A few staged setbacks add drama, and the counter ends on a final score.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: straight into the action, with the counter at zero and a game-style title.
2. 2 to 30 s: each win triggers a sound and the counter ticks up; the pace accelerates.
3. 30 to 50 s: a staged setback (someone overtakes, a deal is lost), then the comeback.
4. Last seconds: the final score on screen, and an optional map or recap.

## Make it yours

Any activity with countable wins works. A sales tool founder films a day of calls, and every booked meeting is a "kill". A support platform counts tickets closed by the AI in an hour. A fitness app founder runs a race and counts every runner passed. Pick wins people can see in the footage, so the counter stays honest. Enter the action at once: a story at the end of the video is the weakest part. This shape was shot in a real program, with a running race as the footage.

## Variations

- Special counters: a second counter for an absurd category (dogs passed, coffees drunk).
- Part series: each part is one session, with the total score carried over.
- Team mode: several people, one counter each, a final leaderboard.

## Why it works

The game frame turns ordinary footage into a contest with a score, so viewers watch to see the final number. The sounds and the counter create a rhythm of small rewards, and the staged setback adds a question about the comeback.

## Watch out

- Use announcer sounds you have the rights to, or similar sounds from the platform library.
- Do not mock the people you pass or beat; blur faces of strangers when needed.
- Keep the counter true to the footage: invented scores break trust.
