# The ten density patterns

Each pattern has a test (how to recognize it), the reason it appears when a model drafts, and the fix. The examples are invented to show the shape; they come from no real page.

## V1. Defensive note

Test: the line explains how a number was counted, what was left out, or why the reader should not misread something, and no reader asked.

Example: under a row of results, "Figures are attributed to named projects, counted in January. Projects under confidentiality are left out of the total."

Why it appears: the drafting model was told never to invent a number, so it shows its care on the page. The care belongs in the source file, not under the proof.

Fix: cut. If the number needs a date or a source, keep it to a few words next to the number ("count of January", "source: press release"). A longer method goes behind a link ("how we count").

Variants: a privacy note under a tool before anyone used it; "this is my working rule" after every threshold; a vendor-bias warning written as a full sentence; "illustrative" explained twice.

## V2. Repetition

Test: the same number, proof or thesis appears in more than two blocks of the page. Search the page for the number.

Example: a headline figure in the hero, in the credentials line, in the stats row, in the note and in the FAQ.

Why it appears: each section was drafted on its own and each one reached for the strongest proof.

Fix: keep the instance with the most context, and one short recall near the call to action at most. In a playbook, a measured number lives in the step where it matters; the table, the limits and the FAQ link to that step.

Variant, the triple opening: the hero subtitle, a summary box and the method lead each say what the page contains. Keep one.

## V3. Announce, recap, comment

Test: delete the sentence; no fact, name, number or instruction is lost.

Examples: "This section covers the three steps." "That is the piece that makes the program work." "In short, the method is a loop."

Fix: cut. A heading already announces; a good paragraph needs no recap.

## V4. Stacked layers

Test: a card or list item carries three or more micro-paragraphs, often with labels ("Format:", "Why:", "Source:").

Why it appears: the model fills every slot of a template for every item.

Fix: one sentence per card with the result and the mechanism. Extra layers go to the case page, or behind a click. Twelve labels on one screen read as a form, not as a story.

## V5. Over-long block

Test: a paragraph over 60 words or a sentence over 28 words that carries one idea. The lint lists them.

Typical shape in a playbook: an evidence paragraph that stacks three to five studies, each with its date, sample and publisher.

Fix: lead with the conclusion in one sentence, keep the strongest study with its source, move the others to a short source list.

## V6. Over-explanation

Test: the text describes what the reader sees (a figure, a table, a button) or explains a term the audience uses every day.

Examples: "The chart above shows views over time." "A hook is the first seconds of a video." (for readers who make videos)

Fix: cut. A caption says what to conclude from the figure, in one line, or nothing.

## V7. Process talk

Test: the line is about how the page or the method was made: a version log, what changed since the last edition, a receipt of a test run, a cell that says "not measured yet", content announced as coming.

Why it appears: the model reports its work to the author, and the report stays on the page.

Fix: version and date in one line at the very end, the changelog on its own page, the receipts in the repository. Never place a version note between the last section and the call to action. Remove "coming soon" until the thing exists.

Variant, machine lines: "Copy this prompt into your assistant:" and a full install command under every step. Say it once at the top; each step keeps a button.

## V8. Too many items

Test: a list, a table or a FAQ where the entries after the fifth repeat the logic of the first five, or answer questions nobody asks.

Fix: keep the entries a reader acts on. A FAQ answer is one or two sentences and a link to the section; it never retells the section.

## V9. Boilerplate

Test: the lint reports the block on three pages or more.

Examples: a commitment paragraph, a rules box, a block of FAQ answers, a closing line with two calls to action.

Fix: one reference page owns the block. Other pages carry one line and a link, or nothing. A component that prints 80 words on eleven pages costs 880 words of reading.

## V10. Second definition

Test: a term defined at its first use is explained again each time it returns.

Fix: define once, link back with the term itself.

## Severity

3: in the first screen, next to a proof, or next to a call to action. 2: in the body of a page a cold visitor reads. 1: deep in a reference document.
