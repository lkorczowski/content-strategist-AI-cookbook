# Subtle foreshadowing: tease the disaster, then the steps

## Shape

A clean step-by-step process plays (a recipe, a setup, a build), but short flashes of a disaster to come are cut between the steps: a hand about to ruin it, a scream, a spill. The steps continue calmly while the flashes grow longer. The disaster finally happens in full at the end, and the reaction closes the video.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: a one-second flash of the disaster about to happen, then back to step one.
2. 2 to 25 s: the steps, each followed by a slightly longer flash of the disaster.
3. 25 to 40 s: the finished result, perfect for a moment.
4. Last seconds: the disaster in full and the reaction of the person who made it.

## Make it yours

One shoot gives you three edits: the clean version, the foreshadowing version and a beauty-shot version. A smart home startup films a perfect evening setup while flashes show the cat walking toward the speaker. An AI presentation tool builds a flawless deck while flashes show a coworker about to click "delete". A coffee subscription brews the perfect cup while flashes show a colleague reaching for the sugar. Film a second camera on the person reacting, so you have the scream. This shape was shot in a real program, as the middle cut of a three-version recipe shoot.

## Variations

- Clean cut: the same steps with no disaster, ending on the positive result.
- Cinematic cut: beauty shots only, no voice.
- Fake-out: the flashes tease a disaster that never happens, and the twist is relief.

## Why it works

The first flash opens a question, "what goes wrong?", that only the end answers, so viewers stay through the steps (Loewenstein 1994). The calm process and the growing flashes build tension that the final reaction releases.

## Watch out

- Stage the disaster safely: no real damage to equipment, food waste kept small, nobody hurt.
- Keep the steps accurate; viewers will try them.
- The disaster must be visible in the first flash, even for a second, or the hook does not land.
