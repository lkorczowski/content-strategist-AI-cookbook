# The famous study, retold

## Shape

The speaker tells a famous experiment as a story: who ran it, what the setup was, what people expected and what really happened. The surprising result lands in the middle. The last part applies the lesson to the viewer's work or life.

## Beats

1. 0 to 3 s: the question the study answers, phrased as a scene ("What happens when a stranger gives a dangerous order to a nurse by phone?").
2. 3 to 40 s: the setup of the experiment, told like a story, with the year.
3. 40 to 90 s: the result, then why it surprised researchers.
4. Last seconds: what it means for the viewer, with one concrete thing to do differently.

## Make it yours

Pick studies that explain how your buyers decide or work. A sales coaching AI retells a study on why rushed people say yes. A productivity app retells the research on unfinished tasks staying in memory. A hiring platform retells a study on bias in interviews. You tell it to camera, and the application at the end connects the study to the problem your product solves. Name the study and the year in the caption so curious viewers can check it.

## Variations

- Myth edition: a famous study that failed to replicate, and what we know now.
- Field test: retell the study, then run a tiny version with your team.
- Series: one study per week on the same theme.

## Why it works

The opening question makes the viewer predict the result, and the gap between their guess and the real answer is what keeps them watching (Loewenstein 1994). A study with a twist is a smart thing to share, which gives social currency to the sharer (Berger 2013).

## Watch out

- Check your retelling against the original paper. Famous studies are often told wrong.
- Mention when a result has been questioned or failed to replicate.
- Keep the application honest: a study on one situation proves little about another.
