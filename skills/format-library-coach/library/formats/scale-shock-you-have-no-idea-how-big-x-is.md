# Scale shock: you have no idea how big X is

## Shape

A voice claims that people underestimate the size of something. Then a series of visual comparisons makes the scale felt, each one bigger than the last, using maps, overlays or familiar objects. The final comparison is the most striking and closes the video.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the claim ("Most people think they understand how big X is. They don't") over the first image.
2. 2 to 30 s: the first comparison, with something the viewer knows well.
3. 30 to 90 s: two or three more comparisons, each one larger, with motion graphics or map overlays.
4. Last seconds: the biggest comparison and one closing line.

## Make it yours

Your market has a scale problem people underestimate: volume of data, emails, hours lost, files stored. You make it visible. An AI email triage tool shows how many emails an average team receives in a year, stacked as paper. A data storage startup shows a year of company files as a physical library. A meeting assistant shows the hours a company spends in meetings as a road trip. Use real numbers from a public source, and let the visuals do the work.

## Variations

- Small scale: how tiny something is (a chip, a cell, a second of computing).
- Map overlay: lay the thing over a country, a city or a building everyone knows (see the second example).
- Time scale: how long something takes, compared with a human life or a familiar event.

## Why it works

Large numbers are abstract, and a familiar comparison suddenly makes them real, which creates a moment of surprise. Each comparison raises the stakes, so the viewer stays for the biggest one. A striking fact is fun to send, which makes the sharer look curious.

## Watch out

- Every number needs a public source. Show it on screen in small text.
- Check your math twice. A wrong comparison gets corrected in the comments and spreads for the wrong reason.
- Use maps, footage and music within their licences and platform rules.
