# Why this does not exist (until now)

## Shape

The video asks why an obvious product has never been made. It explains the technical reason, often with a simple animation or a test on a bench. Then it shows how the team solved the problem, with the product working.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the obvious idea stated as a fact ("If tennis balls had a valve, you could inflate them").
2. 2 to 30 s: the catch: why it fails, explained with one physical or technical reason and a visual.
3. 30 to 80 s: the attempts and the breakthrough, shown on the bench or in the lab.
4. Last 10 to 20 s: the product working in real use, and one line on what it changes.

## Make it yours

This shape is made for deep-tech, hardware and AI founders whose product exists because a hard problem was solved. A battery startup asks why phones cannot charge in one minute, shows the heat problem, then its chemistry. An AI startup asks why doctors still type notes by hand, explains the accuracy problem with medical terms, then shows its model on a real consultation, with consent. A satellite startup asks why farms cannot see their fields every day, shows the cost problem, then its approach. You or an engineer explains: the person who solved it is the most credible voice.

## Variations

- Graveyard opener: show the past attempts that failed before your own.
- Viewer idea: answer a viewer who asked why your product does not exist yet.
- Whiteboard version: draw the problem by hand instead of animating it.

## Why it works

The obvious question creates a gap ("why not?") that the viewer wants closed, and the technical catch deepens it before the answer (Loewenstein 1994). Understanding a hard problem lets the viewer explain it to others, which is social currency (Berger 2013).

## Watch out

- The technical reason must be accurate. Have an engineer check the script.
- Show the product as it works today, with real limits stated.
- Keep trade secrets out of the frame.
