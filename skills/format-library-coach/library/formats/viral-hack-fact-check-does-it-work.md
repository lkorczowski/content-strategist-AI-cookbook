# Viral hack fact-check: does it work?

## Shape

The speaker shows a viral hack or claim, often by playing or screenshotting it. They ask "does it actually work?", check the evidence or test it, and give a clear verdict. The video ends with the safe or realistic version the viewer can use.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the hack on screen and the question ("Have you heard about this hack?").
2. 2 to 30 s: what the hack claims, fairly stated.
3. 30 to 120 s: the evidence or the test, step by step, and the verdict.
4. Last seconds: the safe version that works, or what to do instead.

## Make it yours

Your market has its own viral hacks: growth tricks, AI prompts, productivity rules, pricing tactics. You check them with the expertise you have. An AI writing tool tests the viral prompt that claims to make text undetectable. A cold email platform checks the "send at 6 am" rule against its own aggregated data. A personal finance app checks a budgeting trick that is going around. Your verdict, backed by evidence, positions you as the expert people check with.

## Variations

- Live test: run the hack on camera with your own product or setup.
- Rating version: three hacks, each scored from useless to worth it.
- Reply version: a viewer asks about a hack in the comments, and the video answers it.

## Why it works

The question "does it work?" opens a loop the viewer wants to close before scrolling on (Loewenstein 1994). A trustworthy verdict on a hack people already saw is useful enough to send to the friend who shared it (Berger 2013, practical value).

## Watch out

- State the hack fairly before checking it. A strawman version breaks trust.
- On health, money or safety, never demonstrate a risky hack, and point to a professional.
- Credit the original creator or use the platform's stitch tools, and keep the criticism on the claim.
