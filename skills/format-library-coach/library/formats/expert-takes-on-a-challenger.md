# Expert takes on a challenger

## Shape

The expert accepts a live challenge from an opponent: a match, a debate or a test. Both sit or stand in the same frame, and the contest runs on camera. The outcome is unknown when filming starts, and the video ends on the result.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the challenge stated as a dare, by the opponent or on screen ("Give me one top athlete who is a vegan. One.").
2. 2 to 40 s: the rules, then the first exchanges, with the score or the argument kept visible.
3. 40 to 150 s: the turning point, a strong move or argument, and the opponent's reaction.
4. Last 10 to 20 s: the result, a handshake or a last word, and an on-screen question that asks viewers who won.

## Make it yours

You are the expert, and the challenge tests the skill your product is built on. A founder of an AI sales coaching tool takes on a seasoned sales rep in a live cold-call role play. A founder of a pricing analytics startup debates a skeptical shop owner about raising prices. A founder of a chess or language learning app plays a strong amateur with a handicap. The risk of losing is what makes it watchable: post the video even if you lose, and say what you learned.

## Variations

- Handicap match: the expert plays with a constraint, like the chess game where someone else chooses the moves.
- Street debate: one question, one claim, strangers invited to prove you wrong.
- Rematch series: the loser picks the next challenge.

## Why it works

The open question "who wins?" keeps viewers until the result (Loewenstein 1994). Sharing a clash where one side is proven right makes the sharer look like they picked the right side, which is social currency (Berger 2013).

## Watch out

- The contest must be real. A scripted win, presented as live, damages trust when it comes out.
- Keep the debate about the idea. Personal attacks break platform rules on harassment.
- Get consent and a release from the opponent and from anyone recognizable.
