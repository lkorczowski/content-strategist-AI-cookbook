# Street riddle

## Shape

The interviewer reads the same trick question to several passers-by. They answer fast, often wrong, and the edit lets the viewer watch them think. The video ends when one person finds the right answer, or with the answer on screen.

## Beats

1. 0 to 2 s: the riddle read aloud and written on screen, with the first person already in frame.
2. 2 to 30 s: wrong answers, cut fast, with the pauses and the second thoughts kept in.
3. 30 to 50 s: someone gets close, or argues with a friend, and the tension builds.
4. Last 5 to 10 s: the right answer and the reaction, then the answer explained in one line on screen for viewers who missed it.

## Make it yours

Pick a riddle that touches the logic of your field, so the topic stays near your buyers. An AI tutoring app for maths asks a percentage trick question. A finance tool for small businesses asks a question about compound interest or margins that most people get wrong. A data analytics startup asks a classic probability puzzle. Keep the brand light: the logo in the corner or a caption that says who made the video. The riddle can become a series with the same opening and a new question each week.

## Variations

- Viewer round: stop before the answer and ask viewers to comment theirs, answer in the next video.
- Office riddle: ask colleagues or event attendees instead of strangers.
- Field riddle: a question only people of your trade can solve, asked to people of that trade.

## Why it works

The riddle opens a gap the viewer wants to close, and each wrong answer stretches it a little more (Loewenstein 1994). Viewers try to answer before the strangers, and a riddle they solved is a riddle they send to a friend.

## Watch out

- Make sure the riddle has one clear answer. A debatable riddle turns the comments into a fight about the question.
- Laugh with people. Leave out anyone who looks embarrassed, and get consent from everyone shown.
- Check that the riddle is your own wording or a classic in the public domain.
