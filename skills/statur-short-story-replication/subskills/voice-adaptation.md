# Voice adaptation

The template belongs to the author; the sentences belong to the target creator. Many talking-head authors lean on figures built on negation. They clash with a target voice that asks for affirmative sentences, bans "X, not Y" framing or bans litotes by negation. Keep the function of each figure and change its grammar.

Four common figures, with an invented example of each and its affirmative version:

| Figure | Invented example | Why it clashes | Affirmative version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reversal built on two opposed halves: the closing line flips the motive | "It's not a pricing problem. It's a trust problem." | "X, not Y" framing | State the second half alone, then the image: "It's a trust problem. Pricing is where it shows first." Or split it into two affirmative sentences. |
| Denial joke (apophasis): listing what one is "not saying", then admitting it is the point | "I'm not saying the intern runs the company. I'm just saying the intern has the only admin password." | negation as the engine | "Call it a coincidence if you want. I call it a pattern." |
| A chain of what something lacks | "No roadmap, no budget, no owner." | negation chain | Name what it is, three times: "It runs on hope. It runs on goodwill. It runs on one spreadsheet." |
| A denied twist used as a re-hook | "And that's not even the weird part." | negation | "And it gets weirder." / "And here's the twist." |

## Rules
- Keep the function (reversal, re-hook, denial joke), change the grammar.
- Keep the author's rhythm: the short deadpan answer after a long question survives any voice.
- Read the button aloud in the target voice last; it is where clashes show most.
